Cellar Management

Wine preservation systems for a pub: when a £14 pump beats a £400 argon system

Shaun McManus 3 October 2026

Wine by the glass has a dirty secret: you make your margin on the first two glasses and pour a chunk of it down the sink with the last third of the bottle. The kit that claims to fix this runs from a £14 vacuum pump to a £400 argon system, and whether any of it earns its keep is pure arithmetic, not gadget faith. Here is the arithmetic.

I covered what open wine actually loses and why in an earlier guide, including how long an open bottle really lasts. This one is about the preservation kit itself: what each tier costs, what it can and cannot do, and the two-week test that tells you whether yours is working.

What a dying bottle costs you first

My arithmetic, so check it against your own list. Take a house white that costs you £6.50 a bottle and sells at £5.50 a 175ml glass. A 750ml bottle holds four of those with a splash over. If the last third goes down the sink twice a week on that one line, that is about £2.17 at cost each time, call it £225 a year. Run three slow wine lines the same way and you are pouring away roughly £675 a year at cost, before anyone has done anything wrong. That is the number the kit below has to beat, and notice how small it is per line. Keep it in view, because the sales patter around wine preservation is built on much scarier numbers than yours.

Tier one is free and does most of the work

Before a penny goes on kit: shorten the by-the-glass list to what actually moves, put a date on every bottle the moment it is opened, cap and fridge the whites and rosés between pours, and cut the par level on anything your dead stock report says is sleeping. A dated bottle gets used first or written off properly in the wastage log; an undated one hangs around until somebody smells it. Most pubs that think they have a preservation problem have a list-length problem.

Tier two: the £14 pump

A Vacu Vin style vacuum pump with two stoppers is £14 at UK retail right now. You pump the air out, the seal clicks, the bottle goes back in the fridge. It slows oxidation on still wine, it costs pennies per line, and it pays for itself the first week it saves you one glass. Two limits worth knowing. It does nothing for sparkling, pulling a vacuum on fizz strips the bubbles out, so sparkling gets a pressure stopper instead, which holds the gas in rather than sucking it out. And treat any number-of-days claim on the packet as a ceiling, not a promise: your fridge, your turnover and your wine decide, which is exactly what the date label is for.

Tier three: argon by the glass, the Coravin arithmetic

Coravin’s Timeless Six+ is £399.99 on their UK store, and it works differently: a needle through the cork, argon in, wine out, cork never pulled. The running cost is the part the brochure is quieter about. Their own capsule page prices a 6-pack at £51.99 and says each capsule pours about fifteen 5oz glasses with a Timeless system. That is £8.67 a capsule, or roughly 58p of argon under every glass you pour, my division, their figures.

So the question is never whether a Coravin is clever, it is which bottles can carry 58p a pour plus a £400 box. On a £5.50 glass of house white, forget it, the pump and the date label do that job. Where it earns its keep is the list you currently refuse to open: the £35 bottle nobody risks because one glass sold means four wasted. A needle system keeps the unsold bottle sealed and sellable, so the question changes from “will this bottle die” to “can I charge £9 a glass for it”, and that is a much better question to be asking. If you add premium wines by the glass this way, put them through the till as their own buttons from day one or your variance will be fiction.

Quote-led dispense systems

Above the Coravin sit the blanket-gas and dispense machines you see in wine bars, the Verre de Vin and Enomatic sort. None of the ones I checked publish a UK price; it is all demo and quote. I have said this about stocktaking software and I will say it about wine machines: if a rep cannot put a number in writing before the demo, assume the number needs the demo to survive. For a wet-led pub with a six-line wine list, this tier makes no sense at all.

The two-week test that settles it

Whatever tier you buy, prove it. Two weeks of your wastage log before, two weeks after, same lines, same measures logged every time a bottle dies. If the wine wastage line does not move, the kit was never the problem, the list was, and the fix is fewer lines, not more equipment. Part bottles still get counted either way, by weight, same as the spirits, because a preserved bottle that never gets counted is just a prettier unknown.

Common questions

How long does an open bottle of wine really last in a pub?

Depends on the style, the fridge and whether it was sealed at last orders or at closing. The wine wastage guide covers open-bottle life properly. The honest answer for stock purposes is the date on the label you wrote, counted against the policy you set.

Is a Coravin worth it for a pub?

Only on bottles whose glass price carries about 58p of argon per pour and a £399.99 box, which in practice means a premium by-the-glass list, not house wine. On the house list a £14 pump and a date label do the work at a fraction of the cost.

What about sparkling wine?

Never a vacuum pump, it pulls the fizz straight out. Use a clamp-on sparkling stopper that holds pressure in, keep it cold, and sell it while it still prickles. The wastage log tells you whether your sparkling-by-the-glass is a product or a charity.

Does preservation kit change how I count wine?

No. Open bottles get weighed or tenthed exactly as before, whatever stopper is in the neck. If anything, count more carefully, because preserved bottles live longer and a longer life means more stocktakes straddled.
