Stocktaking & variance

Part bottles are where spirit counts go wrong. Weigh them

Shaun McManus 10 September 2026

Full bottles count themselves. The number that decides whether your spirit stocktake means anything is what's in the open ones, and most bars settle it by holding a bottle up to the light and guessing. A 70cl bottle holds 28 single measures, which at £3.60 a single is about £100 of revenue, so the open shelf behind an ordinary bar is a four-figure sum being estimated by eye in bad light. There's a better way and it costs a tenner: scales.

Here's the maths on both methods, the density sum that turns grams into measures, and which bottles are worth the extra thirty seconds.

What eyeballing actually costs

The standard eyeball method is tenths: hold the bottle up, call it 0.6, move on. Done carefully it's honest to about one tenth either way, and on a clear bottle in decent light that's fine for a lot of jobs. But be clear what one tenth is. On a 70cl bottle it's 70ml, which is 2.8 single measures, which is £10 of revenue at £3.60 a serve. Call forty open bottles a typical back bar, misjudge each by an honest tenth in random directions, and any single count can sit £100 or more adrift of reality without a drop having gone anywhere.

That error doesn't announce itself. It lands in your variance figure looking exactly like missing stock, or like stock you never had. Then next count it unwinds and reverses. If your spirit variance swings from minus £60 to plus £40 and back while draught behaves itself, you probably haven't got a pouring problem, you've got a measuring one. I wrote about telling those apart in the stock loss diagnosis guide, and eyeballed part bottles are the single biggest measuring-error culprit on the wet side.

And some bottles can't be eyeballed at all. Ceramic gins, dark glass, frosted glass, anything with a full-wrap label. For those, guessing isn't even guessing, it's making a number up.

Weighing: the method

Weighing turns the same job into arithmetic. Three steps.

  1. Know the empty weight. Called the tare. Weigh a dead bottle of that product before it goes in the bin, cap on, pourer off, and record it. You do this once per product, ever. Standard bottles barely vary batch to batch.
  2. Weigh the open bottle. Count night, bottle on the scales, read the grams. Cap on, pourer off, same as the tare so you're comparing like with like.
  3. Grams minus tare, divided by density. A 40% ABV spirit is about 0.95 g/ml, because ethanol is lighter than water. So a bottle reading 812g with a 480g tare holds (812 − 480) ÷ 0.95 = 349ml. That's 13.9 single measures. Not "call it half".

Density is the bit people overthink. Ethanol is 0.789 g/ml, water is 1.000, and a 40% spirit lands near 0.95. Cream liqueurs are heavier, call them 1.05. You could chase the exact figure per product off a spec sheet, but a point or two of density is a few millilitres on a part bottle, and consistency count to count matters far more than the third decimal. Pick a figure per product, keep it.

On precision: a £10 kitchen scale resolves 1 gram, which is about 1ml. The unit you sell is 25ml. You're measuring twenty-five times finer than you need, on every bottle, regardless of glass colour or how tired you are. The eyeball on a good night resolves maybe 70ml.

Where tenths still earns its place

I'm not telling you to weigh sixty bottles every Sunday night. Tenths is quicker, and speed is worth real money on a weekly check. The sensible split is by value and by visibility:

  • Weigh: malts, premium gins, cognac, anything north of £20 a bottle at cost, anything opaque, and any line that's shown up in a variance argument. If a figure might end up in a dispute with a stocktaker or an area manager, weigh it, because "I weighed it" ends conversations that "it looked about half" starts.
  • Tenths: house spirits and fast movers on weekly spot checks, where a 70ml error on a bottle you'll empty by Thursday is noise.

A full weighed count monthly with tenths in between suits most wet-led pubs. How often to run each is its own question, covered in how often should a pub do a stocktake.

Common questions

What scales do I need to weigh spirits for a stocktake?

Any kitchen scale that reads to 1 gram and takes 2kg or so, which is a £10 to £15 purchase. One gram is roughly one millilitre of spirit, and a serve is 25ml, so even cheap scales measure about 25 times finer than the unit you sell. Flat platform beats a bowl, and check it against a known weight now and then.

What density figure do I use to convert grams to millilitres?

About 0.95 g/ml for a 40% ABV spirit, because ethanol is lighter than water. Cream liqueurs and heavy syruped liqueurs sit nearer 1.05. Being a point or two out matters far less than being consistent: use the same figure for the same product every count and your movement numbers stay true.

Do I have to weigh every open bottle at every count?

No. Weigh the expensive lines, anything opaque, and anything in dispute, and let tenths cover the cheap fast movers on weekly checks. A full weighed count once a month with tenths in between is a workable rhythm for most wet-led pubs.

How do I find the empty bottle weight?

Weigh a dead bottle of that product before it goes in the bin, with its cap and any pourer removed, and record it once. It barely changes between batches.
