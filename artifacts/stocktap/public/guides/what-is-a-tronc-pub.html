Staff and wages

What is a tronc? How pub tips get shared, the National Insurance it saves, and whether a small pub should bother

Shaun McManus 12 November 2026

What is a tronc? It's a separate pay arrangement for sharing out tips, run by someone other than the employer, called the troncmaster. Run properly, the tips it pays carry income tax but no National Insurance, for you or for your staff.

That last bit is why the word comes up at all. On a busy December the card tips in a pub can run to a few hundred quid a week. Put them through your own payroll and decide yourself who gets what, and HMRC wants employer's and employee's National Insurance on every pound. Hand the sharing-out to an independent troncmaster and it doesn't. Below is how it works, what it saves on real numbers, the tips law you have to follow either way, and the honest answer on whether a small pub should bother.

How a tronc works, in plain English

HMRC's own definitions are short. "A tronc is a special pay arrangement used to distribute tips, gratuities and service charges." "A troncmaster is the person, other than the employer, responsible for arrangements to share tips among employees." That's it. The tips go into a pot, the troncmaster decides how the pot is split, and the money is paid to staff with income tax taken off.

Three things make it work:

  • The troncmaster isn't you. It's usually a senior member of staff, a head bartender or a supervisor the team trusts, or an outside payroll firm. If you, or a director of your company, act as troncmaster, the payments run through your payroll and you're back to the normal rules.
  • You don't decide the split, directly or indirectly. HMRC's guidance says National Insurance isn't due where the "employer does not determine, directly or indirectly, the allocation of those tips". The word that catches people is "indirectly". HMRC gives the example of an employer who requires a points system the employer designed. That counts as the employer deciding, so National Insurance is due.
  • Someone runs PAYE on it. HMRC says "the troncmaster is personally responsible for all aspects of operating a PAYE scheme". The troncmaster can use your payroll software, but the tronc's records have to be kept separate from yours. And unless the arrangement started before 6 April 2004, the employer must tell HMRC the tronc exists.

What it saves, worked out

For 2026 to 2027, employer's National Insurance is 15% on earnings above £96 a week, and the employee rate is 8% on earnings above £242 a week. Anyone working more than about 19 hours a week at the current minimum wage is already over both thresholds before a single tip lands, so for most of your regular staff every extra pound of tips paid through payroll carries the full rates.

The tips law you follow whether you run a tronc or not

Since 1 October 2024 the Employment (Allocation of Tips) Act 2023 has applied to every employer that gets tips. A tronc doesn't change any of this. HMRC's guidance is clear that the new law "does not change how payments are assessed for tax and NICs". What it does change is what you owe your staff:

  • All of it goes to the team. The code says employers must pass on all tips and service charges "without deductions, except in very limited scenarios", and the example it gives is income tax. If you've been taking the card fee off the tips, check that before you carry on.
  • Paid by the end of the following month. A tip left on 23 June has to reach staff by 31 July at the latest.
  • A written policy. Unless tips are only occasional and exceptional, you need a written tipping policy, in plain language, that every worker can see. It covers how tips are accepted and how they're split.
  • Three years of records. Every tip and what each worker got from it, kept for three years. A worker can ask to see them once every three months, and you have four weeks to answer.

One thing is moving. A revised draft code was published in August 2026 under the Employment Rights Act 2025. It adds a duty to consult your staff when you write or review the policy, says the policy should be reviewed at least every three years, and says a simple majority vote shouldn't be the default way to agree it. It's a draft and takes effect subject to Parliament approving it, so treat it as the direction of travel and check gov.uk for the start date.

The draft also covers troncs directly. You can appoint a member of staff, an outside payroll or accountancy firm, or an elected worker as an independent tronc operator, and if you reasonably believe they're running it fairly you're treated as following the code. But the employer "remains liable for the overall fairness and transparency of the scheme". You hand over the sharing-out, not the responsibility.

Cash tips are different

A fiver left on the bar and pocketed by whoever served is outside your payroll entirely. If you have nothing to do with it, the tip is the worker's to declare and you don't take National Insurance on it. The draft code says the same about the tips law: a worker who keeps a cash tip with no employer involvement is outside its scope.

That changes the moment you get involved. If you tell staff how to split the tip jar, or you collect the cash and pay it through payroll, the code says you've likely "exercised control or significant influence". So a tip jar you share out yourself at the end of the night is now your tips, under your policy, with your record-keeping. Decide which way you're running it and write it down. The worst position is a jar that half the team thinks is shared and the other half thinks is theirs.

Should a small pub bother with a tronc?

Here's where I'd be straight with you. A tronc isn't free. Someone has to run a separate PAYE scheme every month, keep separate records and file with HMRC, and if that's an outside firm they'll charge for it. So the saving has to beat the cost and the hassle.

On the table above, a wet-led local taking £50 a week in card tips saves £390 a year of employer's National Insurance. That probably doesn't pay for an outside payroll firm, and it's a lot of admin to put on a bar supervisor for it. A food-led pub taking £300 to £500 a week in card tips and service charge is a different conversation, £2,340 to £3,900 a year on your side and the same again in spirit for the team. That's worth an hour with your accountant.

Two traps if you go ahead. First, a tronc only works if it's independent in practice, not just on paper. If the troncmaster checks the split with you before paying it, or uses a points system you wrote, HMRC can treat it as yours. Second, service charge counts too. If you add a discretionary service charge for parties, it goes through the same rules. My service charge rules guide has the VAT side, which is the other half of that sum.

A one-page policy beats a clever scheme

Whatever you decide, the thing that stops rows is the same: a tipping policy that fits on one sheet, pinned up next to the rota, with last month's pot total written on it in pen. How tips come in (card, cash, service charge). Who shares in them (front of house, kitchen, both). How the split works (hours worked is the simplest and the hardest to argue with). When it's paid. Who runs it. Nobody in pubs seems to bother with the total, but it's the bit that builds trust. When the team can see the pot was £1,240 in November and what their share was, nobody's left wondering.

If you're splitting by hours, you already have the numbers on your rota. The free pub rota maker works out each person's hours for the week, and the cash-up sheet guide shows how to take card tips off the card machine totals, so the tips pot and the till both reconcile every night.

This is a licensee's reading of the HMRC guidance and the draft code, not tax or legal advice. Ask your accountant before you set up a tronc, because the details depend on how your pub is run.

Common questions

What is a tronc?

A tronc is a separate pay arrangement for sharing out tips, gratuities and service charges, run by a troncmaster who is not the employer. The troncmaster decides how the pot is split and runs PAYE on it.

Do you pay National Insurance on tronc payments?

No, as long as the employer does not decide, directly or indirectly, how the tips are allocated. Income tax is still due through PAYE.

Who can be a troncmaster in a pub?

Usually a senior member of staff the team trusts, or an outside payroll or accountancy firm. If the employer or a company director acts as troncmaster, National Insurance applies as normal.

How much National Insurance does a tronc save?

For 2026 to 2027 it saves the employer 15% and the employee 8% on tips that would otherwise go through payroll. On £300 a week of card tips that is £45 a week for the employer and £24 for staff.

Do tips have to be paid to staff by a deadline?

Yes. Under the tips law in force since 1 October 2024, tips must reach staff by the end of the month after the month they were paid, for example a tip left on 23 June by 31 July.

Can I deduct card fees from tips?

The code of practice says tips must be passed on without deductions except in very limited scenarios, with income tax as the example. Check with your accountant before taking card fees off.
