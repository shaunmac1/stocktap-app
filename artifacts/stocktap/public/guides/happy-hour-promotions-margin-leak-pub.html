Money & Margins

Happy hour arithmetic: £1 off a £5.34 pint is 29% off your margin

Shaun McManus 29 September 2026

The quiet Tuesday happy hour feels free. The staff are already paid, the lights are already on, so any extra pint looks like pure gravy. Except the discount doesn't come off your overheads. It comes straight off your GP, the only line that was actually making you money, and most pubs running a promotion have never done the sum on what that costs.

So here's the sum, using the Morning Advertiser's average pint of £5.34.

The discount table

A £5.34 pint is £4.45 before VAT. At a 65% GP the beer in the glass cost you £1.56, which leaves £2.89 of gross profit per pint. Now watch what the discount does.

Price on the boardEx VATGP per pintExtra pints needed to bank the same cash
£5.34 (full price)£4.45£2.89baseline
£4.84 (50p off)£4.03£2.47+17%
£4.34 (£1 off)£3.62£2.06+40%

All author's arithmetic, and the shape holds at your own numbers even if the pounds move. The £1 discount is 19% off the price the customer sees. It's 29% off the margin you keep. And because the discount comes off the ex-VAT price while your cost per pint stays exactly where it was, the volume you need to stand still is always bigger than the discount looks. Forty per cent more pints through the same two hours, every week, before the promotion has made you a penny more than doing nothing.

Who actually gets the discount

The harder question is who those cheap pints go to. Some of them go to new custom the promotion pulled in, which is the point. The rest go to the people who were coming in anyway, and every one of those is a pint you'd have sold at £5.34 now going out at £4.34. A busy happy hour is not evidence the promotion works. It's evidence people like cheap beer. The test is whether the room is fuller than the same two hours last month, and unless your till can show you that by time band, you're guessing.

What a promotion does to your stocktake

This is the bit that catches pubs with good stock control. Say you sell 800 pints a week and 200 of them go out at £1 off. Revenue ex VAT is £3,394 instead of £3,560, cost is unchanged at £1,248, and your achieved GP comes out at 63.2% instead of 65%. Nearly two points gone, with every single pint accounted for.

If the discounted sales went through their own till buttons or a happy hour price level, that's a five-minute explanation on the GP report: price effect, known, move on. If they went through the standard buttons with the price keyed down, or worse, rung in at full price with the difference taken off at the end, your variance report cannot tell a promotion from a leak. You'll spend an evening hunting for stock that was never missing, and the week you finally shrug and accept "the numbers are just off" is the week a real leak starts hiding inside the promotion. The till buttons guide covers the setup; the rule is one button per way of selling, including the cheap way.

The rules in England and Wales

Happy hours are legal here, but they sit inside the mandatory licensing conditions, which every premises licence carries whether you've read them or not. Since May 2014 there have been six of them, and the irresponsible promotions condition is the one that bites promotions. Banned: unlimited or unspecified quantities of alcohol free or for a fixed fee where there's a significant risk to the licensing objectives, so "all you can drink for £15" is a conversation with your licensing officer waiting to happen. Banned: games or activities that require or encourage drinking a quantity within a time limit or drinking as much as possible. Banned: prizes and rewards for drinking, and staff dispensing alcohol directly into a customer's mouth, the dentist's chair. A straightforward time-limited discount on the price of a drink is none of those things.

There's also a floor under how far you can cut. Selling alcohol below the cost of duty plus VAT has been banned since 28 May 2014. On a 4.5% draught pint the duty is about 49.7p at the current rates, so the floor works out around 60p a pint, author's arithmetic from the Home Office formula. Nobody's happy hour goes anywhere near it, but if you ever run a token deal, a wristband deal or a free-pint voucher, the floor is the legal backstop the promotion has to clear.

Scotland is a different country for this

If your pub is in Scotland, the classic happy hour is effectively off the table. The Licensing (Scotland) Act 2005 says a price variation for drinks sold for consumption on the premises can only come into effect at the start of a period of licensed hours, and once it does, no further price change is allowed for 72 hours. Cut the price at 5pm Monday and that's your price until Thursday. It's why Scottish promotions run as week-long deals or permanent price positioning rather than two golden hours, and why a promotion idea borrowed from an English operator's playbook can put a Scottish licence at risk.

If you're going to discount, aim it

I'm not against promotions. I'm against blanket ones, because a blanket happy hour is a price cut for a room that mostly contains your regulars. A discount earns its keep when it has a job: shifting the dead stock that's been sitting on the back shelf since March, filling the two truly dead hours you can name from your till data rather than the two hours the brewery rep suggested, or pulling the afternoon food trade onto a drink they wouldn't otherwise order. Give the deal its own buttons, give it an end date, and read the GP report the week after with the discount's cost written next to whatever it bought you. If you can't say what it bought you, that's your answer.

Run the numbers on your own bar before you run the promotion. Your GP benchmark tells you what a point of margin is worth in your pub, and once you know a point is worth £60 or £80 a week, "it was a busy couple of hours" stops being a good enough reason on its own.

Common questions

Are happy hours legal in the UK?

In England and Wales, yes. Time-limited discounts are fine as long as the promotion isn't irresponsible under the mandatory licensing conditions and nothing sells below the duty plus VAT floor. In Scotland the rules are different: a price change for on-sales can only start at the beginning of licensed hours and must then hold for at least 72 hours, which rules out the classic two-hour happy hour.

How do I work out what a discount actually costs?

Take the discount off the ex-VAT price, not the till price, then compare it to your GP per pint. A £1 discount on a £5.34 pint is 83p off the ex-VAT price, and if your GP was £2.89 a pint it's now £2.06. Divide old GP by new GP for the volume you need: 2.89 over 2.06 is 1.4, so 40% more pints just to bank the same cash.

Why does my GP% drop in promotion weeks with no stock missing?

Because achieved GP% is calculated from what the till took, and the till took less per pint. Sell a quarter of the week's pints at £1 off and your achieved GP% drops nearly two points with every drop accounted for. If promo sales don't go through their own buttons or price level, that price effect is indistinguishable from missing stock.

Which drinks promotions are banned?

The mandatory conditions in England and Wales ban irresponsible promotions: unlimited or unspecified quantities of alcohol for free or a fixed fee where there's a significant risk to the licensing objectives, drinking games and speed-drinking, prizes or rewards for drinking, and staff pouring drinks directly into a customer's mouth. Selling below the cost of duty plus VAT has also been banned since May 2014.
