Staff and wages

Bar staff wages UK: what bar staff get paid in 2026 and from April 2027, tips, and what one bar hour really costs the pub

Shaun McManus 10 November 2026

Bar staff wages UK-wide, in one line: most bar staff are paid at or just above the legal minimum, £12.71 an hour for anyone 21 or over in 2026, £10.85 for 18 to 20 year olds and £8.00 for under-18s, rising to £13.21, £11.27 and £8.31 on 1 April 2027. Job adverts for bartenders on Indeed averaged £14.01 an hour in October 2026. Tips go on top and, by law, all of them go to the staff.

That's the answer whichever side of the bar you're on. I'll take both sides in turn, because the number a member of staff sees on their payslip and the number it costs the pub are a long way apart, and most of the arguments about pay in this trade come from people only looking at one of them.

The legal minimum, now and from April 2027

These are the floors. Nobody can be paid less for the hours they work, whatever their contract says.

What bar staff actually get paid

There isn't one clean official number for bar staff alone that's easy to read, so here's what the published figures say:

  • Job adverts: Indeed's bartender page put the UK average at £14.01 an hour on 18 October 2026, from about 6,000 reported salaries. That's adverts and reports, so it leans towards jobs that are trying to attract people.
  • London: Breakroom's employee reviews put London bar staff at £10.00 to £14.42 an hour (April 2026, 121 reviews). The bottom of that range is below the adult minimum, which tells you it includes younger staff on the lower age rates.
  • The trend: the ONS's payroll figures, reported in October, showed average weekly earnings in hospitality slipping from £354 to £349 between August 2025 and August 2026. That's weekly pay, so fewer hours as much as lower rates.
  • Where it sits: the ONS names bar staff among the lowest-paid occupations, and hospitality had the biggest share of low-paid jobs of any industry in its low pay bulletin.

So in plain terms: an adult on a pub bar is usually on the minimum or up to a pound or two over it, more in London and in busy late-night bars, with supervisors and cellar-trained staff at the top end. And then there are tips.

Tips: the law since October 2024

The Employment (Allocation of Tips) Act came in on 1 October 2024 with a statutory code of practice. In short, the employer has to:

  • pass on all tips and service charges to workers without deductions, except in very limited cases
  • share them fairly and transparently, under a written tips policy staff can see
  • pay them out by the end of the month after the tip was paid, at the latest
  • keep a record for three years, which any worker can ask to see in writing

And one rule that catches people out: tips and service charges don't count towards minimum wage. You can't pay £11 and call the jar the rest. My service charge guide has the VAT and National Insurance side.

The on-costs are holiday (12.07% for irregular hours, on the gov.uk method), employer National Insurance at 15% on earnings over £5,000 a year (£96 a week), and auto-enrolment pension at 3% of qualifying earnings. Three things change it a lot. Employers pay no National Insurance on under-21s. Most small employers can claim up to £10,500 a year of Employment Allowance off their NI bill, which isn't automatic. And lots of short-hours staff each get their own £96 threshold.

That's the real reason a bar job pays what it does. Every hour behind the bar has to sell about seven pints' worth of profit before it's paid for itself, and on a quiet Tuesday afternoon it often doesn't. The free staff hour calculator does the sum for your own week.

The squeeze nobody's planning for: April 2027

This is the bit I'd put on every licensee's list now, not in March. When the minimum goes up 50p, the people just above it don't go up automatically. Say your shift supervisor is on £13.50 today. That's 79p over the minimum. On 1 April 2027 it's 29p over, for someone who locks up, cashes up and runs the shift.

Most pubs fix it in a panic in April, or don't fix it and lose the supervisor in May. The cheaper way is to decide now what gap you want to keep and cost it. Keeping a 75p gap for two supervisors on 30 hours a week each means moving them from £13.50 to £13.96:

  • 46p an hour x 60 hours = £27.60 a week in pay
  • about £35 a week once holiday, NI and pension go on (my rough uplift, about 27%)
  • about 14 pints of gross profit a week, or roughly £1,800 a year

That's not nothing. It's also less than one bad hire and a fortnight of an agency temp. If you know the number in November, you can price for it. My price rise guide shows what 5p or 10p on a pint does to the week.

Deductions: what you can and can't take from wages

Till shortages and breakages come up in every pub group sooner or later. The rules:

  • Only with agreement. Acas says a deduction is only allowed if the contract specifically allows it or the worker agreed in writing beforehand.
  • The 10% cap. For cash shortages and stock deficiencies in retail work, the Employment Rights Act limits deductions to one-tenth of gross pay on a payday, and they have to be made within 12 months. The Act defines retail work as selling or supplying goods or services directly to the public. Selling a pint across a bar fits that wording on my reading, but take advice before relying on it.
  • Uniforms. If staff have to buy their own required uniform and you don't pay them back, gov.uk says it counts against their minimum wage pay. Get it wrong and you've underpaid them.

My view: docking a bar shift for a till that's £10 short rarely fixes anything. A short till is far more often a wrong button, a refund done badly or a tab nobody closed than money in someone's pocket. Find the cause first. My till not matching guide has the usual suspects, and none of them is the person on the till.

If you're looking for a bar job

  • Check the hourly rate against your age band above. It has to be at least that.
  • Ask how tips are shared and ask to see the written policy. A pub that has one has thought about it.
  • Ask whether you're on a rota with set hours or "as and when". Holiday is still owed either way, at 12.07% of hours worked if your hours vary.
  • Ask about cellar training and your personal licence. Both are worth more per hour later, here or anywhere else. My personal licence guide costs it out at about £171 to £219.

This is a licensee's plain-English reading of the rules, not legal or payroll advice. Acas is free to call on anything specific.

Common questions

How much do bar staff get paid in the UK?

Most pub bar staff are paid at or a little above the legal minimum: £12.71 an hour for those aged 21 and over in 2026, £10.85 for 18 to 20 year olds and £8.00 for under-18s. Indeed's bartender page showed an average of £14.01 an hour in October 2026. Tips go on top.

What will bar staff minimum wage be in April 2027?

From 1 April 2027 the National Living Wage for 21 and over rises to £13.21 an hour, the 18 to 20 rate to £11.27 and the 16 to 17 and apprentice rate to £8.31, all up 3.9%.

Do bar staff get to keep their tips?

Yes. Since 1 October 2024 employers must pass on all tips and service charges to workers without deductions, except in very limited cases, share them fairly under a written policy, pay them out by the end of the following month and keep records for three years.

Do tips count towards minimum wage?

No. Tips, gratuities, service charges and cover charges don't count towards minimum wage pay, so the hourly rate must meet the minimum on its own.

Can a pub take till shortages out of wages?

Only if the contract allows it or the worker agreed in writing beforehand. For cash shortages in retail work, deductions are capped at 10% of gross pay per payday. Whether that cap covers bar work is a reading of the Employment Rights Act, so take advice first.
