How many ml in a pint? The UK pint, half and schooner in ml, and how much of it is beer

Shaun McManus 12 November 2026

How many ml in a pint? A UK pint is 568ml, or 568.26ml to be exact. A half is 284ml, a two-thirds schooner is 379ml and a third is 189ml. An American pint is smaller, at 473ml, which is why a US recipe or a US-made glass can catch you out.

That's the answer for the person doing a recipe or a dry-January sum. For anyone behind a bar, the more useful question is how many of those 568ml actually end up in the glass, because that's what decides whether your draught shows a loss or a surplus at stocktake. Both are below.

The UK pint and its parts, in ml

The imperial pint is defined in law as 0.56826125 litres, and it's 20 imperial fluid ounces. Everything else follows from that.

Next to the pint, the packaged sizes look small. A 330ml bottle is 58% of a pint. A 440ml can is 77%. A 500ml bottle of ale is 88%, so a customer who switches from a 500ml bottle to a draught pint is getting 68ml more, about two mouthfuls. And since 2024 shops have been allowed to sell prepacked wine in a 568ml "pint" bottle, though the government was clear that wine by the glass in pubs isn't covered.

What a pub is allowed to serve

Draught beer and cider in a UK pub can only be sold in four quantities: a third, a half, two-thirds, or multiples of a half pint. That's the Trading Standards guidance on Business Companion, last reviewed in September 2026. The rule falls away when the beer is mixed with another drink, so a shandy or a lager top isn't caught.

So you can't sell a 400ml "large glass" of draught lager, or a 500ml stein, however good it looks on the menu. If you want a smaller serve than a pint, it's a half or a two-thirds. If you want a bigger one, it's a pint and a half or two pints. Bottles and cans sold sealed are a different matter, they're sold at whatever is printed on them.

Separately, your licence conditions say a half pint has to be available and customers have to be told it is. The full list, with the spirit and wine rules, is in my spirit measures guide.

Brim glasses, lined glasses and how much is beer

Pint glasses come in two kinds. A brim-measure glass holds exactly a pint when it's full to the top. A lined glass has a line marking the pint, with room above it for the head. Both have to carry the crown stamp, a CE mark or a UKCA mark.

Here's the bit that matters. In a brim glass, the head is part of the 568ml. The BBPA's guidance, published by Cask Marque, is that a measure served with a head should be at least 95% liquid, and that a reasonable request for a top-up should never be refused. Ninety-five per cent of 568ml is 540ml. In a lined glass, all 568ml is liquid and the head sits on top.

That gap shows up in your stock:

Four and a half pints a keg is the difference between a lined-glass pub and a brim-glass pub pouring a good head. That's why a brim-glass pub can count a draught surplus and not understand it, and why a pub that switches to lined glasses can suddenly show a loss on lines that were fine last month. Nothing went missing. The glasses changed. My acceptable variance guide goes through reading a surplus properly, and how many pints in a keg has every keg and cask size.

The jug test nobody does

Here's a five-minute check I'd do on the next stocktake day. Take a measuring jug, pour 568ml of water into one of every type of pint glass you use, and look where it comes to. On a brim glass it should hit the top. On a lined glass it should hit the line. On the branded glasses that came free with a promotion, you might get a surprise, because not every glass in a pub cupboard is a stamped measure, and a glass that holds 600ml to the brim with no line on it isn't a legal pint glass at all.

Then do the same with your halves and your schooners. If a two-thirds glass only fits 379ml at the very brim, staff will be pouring a head into it and short-measuring without meaning to. If it fits 420ml, you're giving away beer on every strong craft pour, which is the most expensive beer you sell. Either way you want to know, and it costs a jug.

Serving smaller measures on purpose

The third and the two-thirds are worth more than people think. A third-pint paddle of three guest ales is a pint in total, priced as a treat, and it moves the cask that's been on the bar four days and needs selling. A schooner of a 6.5% IPA lets someone have the strong beer they want without a full pint of it. At the till the price per ml is usually a touch higher on the smaller measure, so on GP it's no worse than a pint, and it's often better. Common questions

How many ml in a UK pint?

A UK pint is 568ml, or 568.26ml exactly. It is 20 imperial fluid ounces.

How many ml in a half pint?

A half pint is 284ml.

How many ml in a schooner?

A two-thirds pint schooner is 379ml, and a third of a pint is 189ml.

Is a US pint the same as a UK pint?

No. A US pint is 16 US fluid ounces, 473ml, about 95ml less than a UK pint.

How much of a pint has to be beer?

BBPA guidance is that a measure served with a head should be at least 95% liquid, which is 540ml of a 568ml pint in a brim glass. In a lined glass the full 568ml is liquid. Customers can always ask for a top-up.

What sizes can a pub sell draught beer in?

A third, a half, two-thirds or multiples of half a pint. The rule does not apply when the beer is mixed with another drink, such as a shandy.
