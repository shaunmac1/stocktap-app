Money & Margins

How to calculate food GP: the formula, a costed plate, a price-from-cost table and your real kitchen number

Shaun McManus 12 November 2026

To calculate food GP, divide the menu price by 1.2 to take the VAT off, subtract what the food on the plate costs you, then divide what's left by the ex-VAT price. A £15 dish that costs £4.40 to plate is £12.50 ex VAT, leaves £8.10 gross profit, and runs at 64.8% GP.

That's the sum for one dish. The harder part, and the one most kitchens skip, is the same sum done on the whole kitchen with a stocktake, because that's the number that tells you what's really happening to the food you buy. Both are below, with a price-from-cost table you can pin up in the kitchen.

The formula, step by step

  1. Take the VAT off the menu price. Food eaten on the premises is standard-rated, so HMRC gets a sixth of the menu price (20% of the ex-VAT price). Divide by 1.2. £15 becomes £12.50.
  2. Cost the plate. Everything on it, at what you paid on your last invoice, not what it cost when the menu was written.
  3. Gross profit = ex-VAT price minus plate cost. £12.50 minus £4.40 is £8.10.
  4. GP% = gross profit divided by ex-VAT price. £8.10 divided by £12.50 is 64.8%.

The mistake that gives you a lovely number that isn't real is leaving the VAT in. Do the same dish on the menu price and you get £10.60 out of £15, which is 70.7%. That six-point gap is HMRC's money, not yours. If anyone tells you their food runs at 72%, ask whether that's with the VAT in.

Here's an illustrative burger and chips, to show how the pennies add up. These are example prices, so swap in your own from this month's invoices.

At £13.50 on the menu that's £11.25 ex VAT, £7.95 gross profit and 70.7% GP. Leave off the last three lines because they "don't really count" and you'd think it was 76%. Those little bits are where theoretical GP and real GP part company.

Three rules that keep the costing honest:

  • Cost the cooked, trimmed portion, not the raw weight bought. A joint loses weight in the oven and a whole salmon loses weight on the board. If you buy 1kg and serve 700g, every portion costs a third more than the invoice price per kilo says.
  • Weigh what the kitchen actually serves. Weigh ten portions of chips on a busy night and take the average. If the spec is 200g and the average is 240g, your chips cost 20% more than your menu card thinks.
  • Re-cost when the invoice changes, not when the menu changes. Food inflation was 1.3% in the year to September 2026 on the ONS figures, which is gentle, but single lines move far more than the average. One dear supplier price on your best seller can take two points off the whole kitchen.

The multiplier is 1.2 divided by (1 minus the GP target). Then round to a price that looks like a menu price, and round up, not down. A £15.09 dish goes on at £15.25 or £15.50, never £14.95.

What target? The most recent segmented benchmark for UK pubs, the UKHospitality and Christie & Co report published in 2022, had food GP averaging 66% across the survey, with community locals and food-led pubs both on 65%. I'd call 60% to 68% doing well depending on your menu and prices. The full breakdown is in my pub GP benchmarks guide. Not every dish has to hit the target. A steak at 55% that sells with a bottle of wine is fine. Chips at 55% are not.

The number that matters: your kitchen's actual GP

Dish costings give you your theoretical GP, what the kitchen should make if every plate goes out to spec and nothing is wasted. Your actual GP comes from a food stocktake, and it's the only figure that tells the truth:

Food cost = opening food stock + food purchases − closing food stock. Then actual GP% = (food sales ex VAT − food cost) ÷ food sales ex VAT.

Say your menu costings average 68%, food sales are £3,000 a week ex VAT, and a four-week stocktake shows food cost of £4,320 on £12,000 of sales. That's 64% actual. The four-point gap is £480 over the four weeks, £120 a week, £6,240 a year. That money went somewhere, and it's nearly always the same places:

  • Waste. WRAP's study of hospitality food waste found 45% of it happened in preparation, 34% came back on customers' plates and 21% was spoilage. It's older data, from 2013, but the split is still the best guide to where to look first.
  • Portion creep. The chips test above.
  • Staff food and comps that never go through the till. Feeding the team is fine. Not recording it means it shows up as a mystery loss.
  • Bar to kitchen transfers. The wine in the sauce, the lemons, the ale in the batter. My transfers guide covers how to book them so neither side's GP lies.

How to count the kitchen without it taking all morning is in my kitchen stocktake guide. Monthly is enough for most pubs. Weekly if you're chasing a problem.

The one-line weekly check

Between stocktakes there's a quick sum nobody seems to do. Every Monday, add up the food invoices for last week and divide by last week's food sales ex VAT. If the answer is around 0.32 to 0.36, you're in the right area for a 64% to 68% kitchen. If it jumps to 0.42 one week, something happened, a big delivery that's sitting in the freezer, a price rise, or a bad week for waste. It's not as accurate as a stocktake because stock levels move, but it takes two minutes and it catches a problem three weeks before the stocktake would.

And if you're thinking of paying the chef a bonus on GP, get the actual number measured for two or three months first. My kitchen GP bonus guide explains why a GP-only bonus can pay for smaller portions, and how to stop that.

Common questions

How do you calculate food GP?

Divide the menu price by 1.2 to remove VAT, subtract the cost of the food on the plate, then divide the gross profit by the ex-VAT price. A £15 dish costing £4.40 is £12.50 ex VAT, £8.10 gross profit, 64.8% GP.

What is a good food GP for a pub?

The most recent segmented UK benchmark, published in 2022, had pub food GP averaging 66%, with community locals and food-led pubs on 65%. Between 60% and 68% is doing well for most pub menus.

How do I price a dish to make 65% GP?

Multiply the plate cost by 3.43 to get a menu price including VAT. A £4.40 plate needs about £15.09, so price it at £15.25 or £15.50.

Should food GP include VAT?

No. Calculate it on the ex-VAT price. Leaving the VAT in makes a 64.8% dish look like 70.7%.

What is the difference between theoretical and actual food GP?

Theoretical GP comes from dish costings. Actual GP comes from a food stocktake: opening stock plus purchases minus closing stock, against food sales ex VAT. The gap is usually waste, portion creep, unrecorded staff food and transfers.
