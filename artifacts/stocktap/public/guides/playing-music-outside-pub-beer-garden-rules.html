Running the pub

Music in the beer garden: what your licence lets you play outside, and what gets you an abatement notice

Shaun McManus 19 October 2026

In England and Wales, if your pub is licensed to sell alcohol for drinking on the premises and it's open for that, playing recorded music or putting on live music doesn't need any licence permission between 8am and 11pm, as long as an amplified audience is no bigger than 500. Most beer gardens count. Any old condition on your licence about music doesn't apply in that window either, unless a review has put it back. But 11pm is a hard stop, and none of it protects you from a noise nuisance complaint, which on business premises carries an unlimited fine.

Every summer the same thing happens. Someone puts a speaker out by the smoking shelter, the garden fills up, and by half nine there's a neighbour on the phone to the council. Half the licensees I know think music outside needs special permission. The other half think the licence covers them whatever. Both are wrong, in different directions, so here's what the law actually says, where it stops, and how to set the garden up so it never gets as far as a letter.

Quick scope note: this is England and Wales. Scotland licenses entertainment under its own Act, and none of the 11pm and 500-person rules below carry over.

The government's own summary is short. A licence isn't needed for live music or recorded music if it takes place between 8am and 11pm, at alcohol on-licensed premises, with an audience of no more than 500. You also don't need one for unamplified live music anywhere in those hours, or for amplified live music at a workplace in those hours for up to 500 people.

Whether the garden counts is the first thing to check. If it's inside the red line on your licence plan, it's part of the licensed premises. If it isn't, licensing solicitors Poppleston Allen's view in the Morning Advertiser is that it's still a workplace, and the government guidance treats workplace as a very wide term that can include outdoor space, so a live act still gets the 11pm relaxation. Recorded music is the gap. That exemption is tied to the alcohol-licensed premises, so a speaker in a car park or a field that isn't on your plan is a different question.

There's also an exemption for background or incidental music, music that isn't really the entertainment in its own right. A quiet playlist under the chatter can fall into it. I wouldn't rely on that argument after 11pm in a garden backing onto houses, which is exactly where someone will test it.

Those music conditions on your licence: asleep, not dead

Plenty of older licences carry conditions like "no music in the external areas" or "doors and windows closed during regulated entertainment". Since April 2015, section 177A of the Licensing Act 2003 says that any condition on your licence relating to live or recorded music has no effect while the music falls inside that 8am to 11pm, under-500, open-for-alcohol window.

The catch is the review. If your licence is reviewed, the council can add a music condition back, or mark an existing one so that 177A no longer switches it off. And anyone can apply for a review, including the neighbour behind your garden wall. So the condition being asleep is not a licence to ignore it. Push your luck in July and you can end up with a condition that does bite, permanently, on top of the conditions you started with.

The law that doesn't care what your licence says

This is the bit people miss. Licensing and noise are two separate systems. You can be fully within your licence and still be committing a statutory nuisance.

Statutory nuisance. Under the Environmental Protection Act 1990, if the council is satisfied that noise from your premises is a statutory nuisance, it serves an abatement notice, or for noise it can try to persuade you to sort it first and must serve the notice if that hasn't worked within seven days. Breaking an abatement notice is a criminal offence. On industrial, trade or business premises, which a pub is, the fine has no upper limit. There is a defence for business premises: that you used the best practicable means to prevent or counteract the noise. Keep that phrase in mind, because everything in the set-up section below is about being able to prove it.

The Noise Act at night. The Noise Act 1996 lets the council act on complaints about noise during night hours, which it defines as 11pm to 7am, and since 2006 in England that has included licensed premises. An officer can serve a warning notice, and if the noise then goes over the permitted level measured from inside the complainant's home, the person responsible commits an offence. The fixed penalty for licensed premises is £500, and the maximum fine in court is level 5.

Most councils start with a conversation. North Yorkshire Council, for one, tells residents to approach the pub directly first and give it a chance to put things right before reporting it. That first knock on the door is the cheapest moment you'll ever get to fix it, so treat it that way.

A free decibel app on your phone won't stand up in court, but it's handy for keeping a rough record of the setting you used, held at the same spot each time. The incident log is the right place for the complaint notes, alongside everything else you already write down.

Smokers, glass collection and people leaving cause as many complaints as the music does. A speaker playing quietly at half nine gets blamed for the shouting at ten past eleven. Close the garden on time and someone walks it, and half the music complaints go away with it.

The licence that pays the musicians

Everything above is about licensing and noise. It has nothing to do with copyright. Playing recorded music to customers, inside or out, still needs a music licence, which for most pubs is TheMusicLicence from PPL PRS. A personal streaming account on your phone doesn't cover the garden any more than it covers the bar. If you've started playing music somewhere you didn't before, check what you've declared still matches what you play. The 2026 costs are in the pub music licence guide.

Is it worth it?

Only if it sells something. The research on music and drinking is smaller than people think, and I went through it study by study in does background music make people drink more. A garden with a gentle playlist on a sunny Saturday probably holds people for another round. A garden with a live act on a Tuesday is a different sum, because the act costs money and the trade has to come from somewhere. Check it on your own figures: the till by hour on music days against the same days without, and your line counts for the week. If nothing moves, you've been paying a complaint risk for nothing.

My take

The law is more generous than most licensees realise. Up to 11pm, a pub garden can have music on without asking anyone's permission, and most old music conditions are switched off. What it isn't is a free pass. The neighbour can still get you an abatement notice with no cap on the fine, or a review that writes the condition back in for good. Aim the speaker at your own wall, keep it at a level you'd be happy hearing from your own bedroom, switch it off before 11, and write down what you did. Then nobody has a case.

This is a licensee's reading of the rules, not legal advice. If a review is on the table, get a licensing solicitor.

Common questions

Can a pub play music in the beer garden?

In England and Wales, yes, between 8am and 11pm, if the pub is licensed for alcohol on the premises and open for it, and an amplified audience is 500 or fewer. No licence permission is needed in that window, but noise nuisance law and your music copyright licence still apply.

What time does music have to stop outside a pub?

The licensing exemption ends at 11pm. After that, music needs to be authorised by your premises licence or a temporary event notice, and the Noise Act's night hours run from 11pm to 7am. Plenty of pubs stop outside music earlier, around half ten, to avoid complaints.

My licence says no music outside. Does that still apply?

Between 8am and 11pm, a condition about live or recorded music usually has no effect under section 177A of the Licensing Act 2003, as long as the pub is open for alcohol and the audience is 500 or fewer. A licence review can switch it back on, and anyone, including a neighbour, can apply for one.

What happens if a neighbour complains about pub music?

The council can investigate under the Environmental Protection Act 1990 and serve an abatement notice if it finds a statutory nuisance. Breaching one on business premises carries an unlimited fine. At night, from 11pm to 7am, the Noise Act 1996 allows a warning notice and a £500 fixed penalty for licensed premises.

Do I need a music licence to play music outside my pub?

Yes. Playing recorded music to customers in the garden needs the same copyright licence as inside, usually TheMusicLicence from PPL PRS. A personal streaming subscription doesn't cover it.
